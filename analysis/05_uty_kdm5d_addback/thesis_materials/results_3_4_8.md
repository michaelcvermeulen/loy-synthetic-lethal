### 3.4.8 Transcriptional responses to UTY and KDM5D re-expression

#### Experimental design and verification of re-expression

We next asked whether re-expression of two Y-linked chromatin regulators altered the transcriptional state of the established HT1080 Y-depleted population. Empty-vector, UTY, KDM5D and combined UTY–KDM5D populations were generated by separate transductions from the same starting Y-depleted pool. RNA was collected from each established population on three matched collection occasions. The three samples per population therefore measured the persistence of differences between established populations; they were not three independently performed transductions. The Y-retaining sgCTRL and untransduced Y-depleted populations provided contextual references ([experimental design and expression, panel a](figures/chapter3/ch03_main_ht1080_addback_design_expression.pdf)).

Western blotting detected the expected UTY and KDM5D abundance in the corresponding construct populations across samples. RNA-seq likewise showed high UTY expression in the UTY and combined populations and high KDM5D expression in the KDM5D and combined populations (panel c). Low KDM5D RNA signal was also present in the UTY population, but remained far below that in the two KDM5D-containing populations. The original Western images and lane map will replace the reserved panel b after source-image verification.

#### Re-expression was associated with two principal plasmid-population states

For the primary expression analysis, all six populations were filtered and normalised together. Y-linked and pseudoautosomal features were excluded from the primary background, leaving 23,992 expressed autosomal and non-pseudoautosomal X-linked genes. An unadjusted principal-component analysis placed the empty-vector and KDM5D populations together and the UTY and combined populations together. PC1 and PC2 explained 37.3% and 14.5% of expression variance, respectively (panel d). Samples from the three collection occasions remained close within each population.

This structure was reflected in direct gene-level comparisons. Using a collection-occasion-blocked limma-voom model, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction and a descriptive absolute log2 fold-change filter of 0.5, UTY versus empty vector identified 1,155 genes and combined UTY–KDM5D versus empty vector identified 2,153 genes. KDM5D versus empty vector identified no genes under these criteria. The combined and UTY populations also had no selected genes in their direct comparison, whereas the combined and KDM5D populations differed at 2,868 genes. These counts describe evidence against a zero effect followed by an effect-size filter; they do not control the false-discovery rate for effects exceeding 0.5. A formal TREAT analysis at the same fold-change threshold retained 240 genes for UTY versus vector, 197 for combined versus vector and none for KDM5D versus vector ([sensitivity analysis](figures/chapter3/ch03_supp_ht1080_addback_sensitivity.pdf)).

The naive Y-depleted and empty-vector populations differed at 4,032 genes under the descriptive criteria. These populations also differed in transduction and selection history and in their residual Y-linked expression. The empty-vector state therefore provided the required reference for add-back comparisons but could not be treated as interchangeable with the untransduced Y-depleted state. Likewise, the similarity of the two UTY-containing populations supported a UTY-associated population difference but did not provide independent replication of the UTY transduction.

#### UTY-containing populations shifted a frozen LOY signature towards sgCTRL

To assess reversal without selecting genes from the May add-back data, we used a 100-gene signature frozen from the August comparison of the established Y-depleted and sgCTRL populations. The signature contained 50 genes with higher and 50 with lower expression in Y-depleted cells; all were represented in the May count matrix. Because August and May sampled the same original baseline populations, this separation prevented May-dependent gene selection but did not constitute validation in an independently generated model.

Within-sample percentile-rank scores reproduced the higher August-defined LOY state in the May naive Y-depleted population. Mean scores were −0.201 in sgCTRL, 0.153 in naive Y-depleted and 0.038 in the empty-vector population ([baseline reversal, panel a](figures/chapter3/ch03_main_ht1080_addback_baseline_reversal.pdf)). UTY and combined re-expression shifted the score below the empty-vector value, to −0.070 and −0.047, respectively, whereas the KDM5D population remained at 0.069. In the blocked score model, the differences relative to empty vector were −0.108 for UTY (95% CI −0.149 to −0.067; P = 1.6 × 10^-4), 0.031 for KDM5D (95% CI −0.011 to 0.072; P = 0.13) and −0.085 for combined re-expression (95% CI −0.127 to −0.044; P = 9.7 × 10^-4; panel b). The combined population did not differ detectably from UTY alone on this score (difference 0.023, 95% CI −0.018 to 0.064; P = 0.25).

Directional rotation tests gave concordant gene-set evidence. For UTY versus vector, August-down genes increased and August-up genes decreased after correction across the 12 prespecified signature-arm tests (FDR = 5.1 × 10^-4 and 0.0032, respectively; panel d). The combined population showed the same corrective directions (FDR = 5.1 × 10^-4 and 0.0477). Neither signature arm passed the corrected threshold for KDM5D versus vector. Combined re-expression differed from KDM5D alone in both corrective directions but not from UTY alone.

The gene-level trajectories were heterogeneous (panel c). Seventy-five frozen-signature genes passed the May primary expression filter. Relative to the empty-vector distance from sgCTRL, 49 of 75 had group-mean expression closer to sgCTRL after UTY re-expression, 59 of 75 after combined re-expression and 26 of 75 after KDM5D re-expression. These descriptive counts include genes that crossed the sgCTRL mean and do not assign a binary rescue state. They also share the empty-vector estimate and therefore complement, rather than replace, the direct contrast and rotation-test results.

The untransduced Y-depleted population provided a construct-free comparison for the same empty-vector reference. On the frozen signature it moved in the opposite direction to the UTY-containing populations. Its score was 0.114 higher than the empty-vector score (95% CI 0.073 to 0.156; P = 1.1 × 10^-4), and the August-up arm increased (FDR = 9.8 × 10^-6). The reduced signature score in UTY-containing populations was therefore not a property shared by every population outside the empty-vector–KDM5D pair.

#### Pathway-level differences were largely shared with the untransduced Y-depleted population

Competitive CAMERA tests were applied to MSigDB Hallmark, Reactome and GO Biological Process gene sets using the same blocked model ([pathway sensitivity](figures/chapter3/ch03_supp_ht1080_addback_pathway_sensitivity.pdf)). With the conventional fixed inter-gene correlation of 0.01, UTY versus empty vector identified 19 of 50 Hallmark, 356 of 1,340 Reactome and 275 of 4,684 GO Biological Process sets at within-collection FDR <0.05. The strongest were lower proliferation, E2F-target, mitotic and ribosome-biogenesis programmes. Combined re-expression gave a similar profile, whereas KDM5D versus vector identified 7, 62 and 19 sets, led by lower oxidative-phosphorylation and respiratory-chain programmes.

These pathway shifts were not specific to the constructs. The untransduced Y-depleted population, compared with the same empty-vector population, gave 18 Hallmark, 546 Reactome and 538 GO Biological Process sets with the same leading programmes. Signed CAMERA statistics for UTY versus vector correlated closely with those for naive Y-depleted versus vector (Spearman ρ = 0.93, 0.88 and 0.79 for Hallmark, Reactome and GO Biological Process). Most UTY-associated sets were also significant, in the same direction, for the naive Y-depleted population: 14 of 19 Hallmark, 311 of 356 Reactome and 212 of 275 GO Biological Process sets. None were significant in the opposite direction. The combined population showed the same concordance (ρ = 0.96, 0.95 and 0.88), whereas KDM5D versus vector did not (ρ = 0.24, 0.47 and 0.15). The dominant pathway-level signal therefore distinguished the empty-vector and KDM5D populations from the other Y-depleted populations. It could not be attributed to UTY re-expression.

The number of significant pathways also depended strongly on the assumed inter-gene correlation. Estimated within-set correlations had a median of 0.034 across all tests: about 0.05–0.07 for Hallmark and Reactome sets and 0.03 for GO Biological Process sets. At a fixed correlation of 0.03, UTY versus vector retained 6 Hallmark, 228 Reactome and 2 GO Biological Process sets, and KDM5D versus vector retained 1, 11 and 1. When correlations were estimated separately for each set, no set in any contrast passed FDR <0.05. The combined and UTY populations had no selected genes in their direct comparison, yet at the 0.01 setting they differed in 484 Reactome sets, mostly lower translation programmes in the combined population. This illustrates the sensitivity of the conventional setting to large, highly co-regulated sets. Seventeen small sets remained significant for UTY versus vector at both fixed settings without a same-direction result for naive Y-depleted cells. These were mainly interleukin-6-family, collagen-receptor, angiogenesis and coagulation sets with increased expression, and 14 also changed in the same direction for combined versus KDM5D. They are retained as hypothesis-generating observations rather than UTY-specific pathway findings.

Together, these analyses show that the two UTY-containing populations occupied a related transcriptional state and shifted both arms of a predefined LOY-associated signature towards the Y-retaining reference. KDM5D re-expression alone produced neither a detectable genome-wide gene-level response nor a corrective shift in the frozen signature under the retained analyses. The results support partial molecular reversal associated with UTY-containing populations. The broader pathway differences did not show the same specificity. They were largely reproduced by the untransduced Y-depleted population and depended on the inter-gene correlation assumed by the competitive test, so they are not interpreted as UTY-regulated programmes. Since each construct was represented by one established population, they do not separate the construct effect from all stable differences acquired during population derivation, demonstrate catalytic mechanism, or establish rescue of a genetic dependency. Functional consequences are addressed separately in Chapter 4.
